How spaghetti breaks

Squeeze both ends of a strand with a force and watch it bow. Colour shows bending stress. Past the breaking force, the strand cracks where stress peaks, and the snap sends a wave that can break it again.

Model: a pinned strand under end load is an Euler elastica, solved numerically. Bending stress is σ = E·κ·z (κ is curvature, z the distance from the centre line), minus the small axial term F/A. Breaking stress is E × breaking strain; 0.6 % reproduces the paper’s ≈8 m⁻¹ limit curvature for a 1.5 mm strand. After the first crack, the released end carries a bending wave that amplifies curvature by up to 1.428 (Audoly and Neukirch, as verified by Zhang et al., 2021). Further cracks are placed where 1.428 × local stress still exceeds the breaking stress. This is a simplified one-generation model. The asymmetry slider makes the right half of the strand weaker, so the first crack moves off-centre. The two legs then race to their second crack (wave arrival time grows with the square of the distance); if one side’s wave arrives more than 20 % later, that leg survives and you get three pieces. The 20 % window is my own tuning, not from the paper. Twist follows Heisser et al. (PNAS 2018). Twisting adds shear stress, so the strand fails when (E·κ·r)² + 2(μ·r·θ′)² reaches (E × breaking strain)², which is their effective-stress rule rescaled to this page’s breaking strain, and so at lower curvature (μ is the shear modulus, θ′ the twist per length). The twist wave dies out quickly, so a second crack has to come from bending alone, amplified by 1.428. Once the first crack happens below about 0.70 of the zero-twist curvature, no second crack is possible and the strand snaps in two. Ignoring the minimum fragment length, that happens at 71 % of the pure-twist breaking angle; with it the threshold is a little higher, and the readout computes it. Second cracks also need fragments of at least one eighth of the length (their measured 3 cm for 240 mm). I ignore twist’s effect on the bent shape. Heisser et al. used 1.4 mm strands that survived about 360° of pure twist; a breaking strain near 1.0 % here reproduces that. End conditions use equivalent columns. Fixed–free is half of a pinned–pinned strand of twice the length. Fixed–fixed is a pinned–pinned strand of half the length in the middle, with a clamped quarter at each end. Fixed–pinned is solved by shooting for the elastica whose end tangent is parallel to the line between the ends. Buckling loads are π²EI/(KL)² with K = 1, 0.5, 0.699 and 2. In the dynamic view each leg obeys EI·κ′′′′ + ρA·κᵗᵗ = 0 with κ = 0 at the new free end, and I use the similarity solution κ = κ₀·2·FresnelS(ξ/√(2π)) with the local κ₀ (the paper’s uniform-curvature solution applied locally). It ignores the far boundaries and wave reflections, so it is only valid for the first milliseconds. Second cracks are placed by the static estimate above and fire when the curvature peak (1.428 κ₀) reaches them, at t = (d·(ρA/EI)^¼ / 2√π)² for distance d from the first crack. The legs are drawn anchored at their supports, with no rigid-body motion. Strand thickness is exaggerated in the drawing.
